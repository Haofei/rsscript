# I'm not the only one: MoonBit and the shape of an AI-native language

[Post 4](04-why-no-one-built-this-before.md) made a prediction. After arguing that the moment for a smaller, AI-shaped systems language is a recent and real intersection of conditions, I wrote: *there will be a "simpler X for AI" project from a well-resourced team within two years.* I want to update that prediction, because one is already here, it's further along than RSScript, and it's more deliberately AI-native than I expected anything to be this soon. It's called MoonBit.

This post is about what a serious peer tells you. Both the parts where we independently arrived at the same answer — which is the strongest evidence I have that the direction is real and not just a story I told myself from the narrow vantage of reviewing too much generated Rust — and the parts where we bet differently, which is where the genuinely open design questions live. I'm going to try hard not to do the thing where you describe a competitor only to declare yourself the winner. MoonBit is ahead of me, and the honest read of it is more interesting than a comparison I rig.

## What MoonBit is

MoonBit is an AI-native general-purpose language with its own compiler, build system (`moon`), and package registry, targeting WebAssembly, JavaScript, and native code. It's backed by a research institute, it's in a stabilizing beta, and it's aiming at a 1.0 this year. By every measure of maturity — toolchain, ecosystem, documentation, team size, funding — it is well ahead of where RSScript is.

The part that matters for this series is the framing, and it's worth being precise about how aggressive it is. MoonBit is not a nice general-purpose language that happens to be convenient for AI. It was *designed* as an AI codegen target, on purpose, with specific compiler and toolchain decisions justified by how transformers generate tokens. They even have a language construct — a `declare` form — for writing the *specification* of code you want an AI to fill in, which is the "spec is the contract, the model writes the body" idea promoted to a keyword. That's the same thesis as these posts, arrived at independently, by a team with very different resources, a different backend, and a different syntactic heritage. When two efforts that share none of their implementation share their *conclusions*, the conclusions are the thing to look at.

## Where we converged, without coordinating

Three places, and the first one genuinely surprised me.

**Flat, top-level structure.** MoonBit deliberately eliminates nested code blocks and lets a type's methods be defined linearly, outside any enclosing block. Their stated reason is mechanical and specific: nesting forces the model to jump around the file to resolve a definition — to hold an outer block open in its head while it generates an inner one — which costs accuracy and, just as importantly, wrecks the model's ability to reuse its cached attention over the prefix. Flat code generates left-to-right without backtracking, which is faster and more reliable for a transformer.

RSScript arrived at the *same shape* — methods are top-level `fn Type.method(...)`, there is no `impl` block holding a method body off somewhere apart from its type — but for a completely different stated reason: a human reviewer shouldn't have to scroll to another block to learn what a call does. One project optimized for KV-cache reuse. The other optimized for reviewer attention. Same structural conclusion: *don't make the reader — human or model — backtrack.*

That convergence was striking enough that I wrote it into RSScript's constitution as its own article, a sibling to the explicitness budget from [the last post but one](05-explicit-is-a-budget.md). The framing it forced on me is one I now think is load-bearing for the whole category: the source text has *two* readers, the human reviewer and the model that produced it, and they pay in different currencies. What a reviewer pays in attention, a model pays in tokens and cache. Most of the time the two interests point the same way — flat, local, explicit code is cheaper for both — which is why a language designed honestly for one tends to serve the other. MoonBit started from the model's cost and landed somewhere a reviewer likes. I started from the reviewer's cost and landed somewhere a model likes. We met in the middle because the middle is where the actual answer is.

**Fits-in-context as the distribution model.** Both projects treat "put the spec in the prompt" as a primary path rather than an afterthought, and both keep the surface small enough that it's actually possible. This was [post 3 and 4](03-less-is-more-20-80-rust.md)'s central distribution bet, and seeing it load-bearing for a second, unrelated team is a small but real confirmation that it isn't a quirk of my situation.

**The compiler as a decoding oracle.** This is the big one, and it's the reason I bumped this whole post up the queue. In the [last post](06-teaching-a-language-that-doesnt-exist.md) I described constrained decoding — having the compiler tell the model which next tokens are legal, so an invalid program becomes literally impossible to emit — as a design I had drafted but not shipped. MoonBit has *shipped* it. They call it local and global sampling: a local check for syntactic validity and a global one for semantic validity, run during generation, catching invalid tokens and steering the model back, at a reported overhead of a few percent for a large jump in how often the generated code compiles on the first try. They built the exact layer I was calling a frontier. That is the single strongest piece of evidence I have that the layer is real and worth building — not a thing I talked myself into about my own checker, but a thing a more mature team independently decided was worth shipping. When the most speculative item on your roadmap turns out to be someone else's released feature, you should update hard toward "this direction is correct," and I have.

## Where we bet differently

**The backend.** This is the cleanest divergence and the place [post 4](04-why-no-one-built-this-before.md)'s argument is most directly on the line. MoonBit built its own backend — its own code generator targeting WASM, JS, and native. RSScript's entire post 4 thesis was *don't build a backend; lower to Rust and inherit fifteen years of its optimizer, its platform support, its debuggers, all of it, for free.* So one of us is leaving value on the table, and intellectual honesty requires admitting it is not obviously MoonBit. Their own backend buys them WebAssembly and JavaScript reach that a lower-to-Rust language simply does not have, and a degree of control over the generated output that I gave away on purpose. My bet is still that for *systems-shaped application code specifically*, inheriting Rust's backend is the higher-leverage move, and that building your own is the trap that quietly sank most of the shelf in post 4 — they spent their decade on codegen instead of on the front end where the value lives. But MoonBit is a real, well-executed counterexample to "you should never build your own backend." The honest statement isn't "I'm right and they're wrong." It's "we made different bets with our eyes open, and the bets serve different targets — their reach, my leverage."

**How much to infer.** MoonBit infers a lot — types, error propagation, much of the boilerplate — for writer ergonomics. That's a reasonable, even obvious, choice for a general-purpose language. RSScript deliberately refuses inference in *public contracts*: the signature is a review contract, and a contract you have to run inference to read is not one a reviewer can trust at a glance. This is precisely the *review-first versus writer-first* axis from post 4, and watching it play out is clarifying. Even a language as deliberately AI-native as MoonBit still optimizes more for the writer than the reviewer at the contract boundary — not because its designers are careless, but because it's general-purpose and isn't carrying "review is the bottleneck" as a founding mandate the way RSScript is. That's not a criticism. It's a different objective function. But it does confirm post 4's claim that the review-first axis is still, even now, mostly unworked — the AI-native peer that showed up didn't take that seat either.

**Where even we agree at the boundary.** Here's the detail I find most telling. MoonBit makes error *types* explicit in the function signature even while inferring error *propagation* through the body — a function declares it can raise a given error, but you don't hand-thread the rethrows. RSScript does the structurally identical thing with effects: the contract is explicit at the signature, the mechanics are silent in the body. Two AI-native languages, built by people who have never spoken, independently landing on *be explicit at the boundary, infer in the interior* — that feels much less like coincidence and much more like the actual stable answer to [post 5](05-explicit-is-a-budget.md)'s question of where to spend the explicitness budget. If I had to bet on one design principle from this whole series surviving into whatever language eventually wins this category, it would be that one, and it would be partly because I watched someone else arrive at it from the opposite direction.

## What a peer this strong actually means

It would be easy to read "someone is doing this, better-resourced and further along" as bad news for a small project. I read it as close to the best news available, for a reason post 4 set up directly.

The whole risk in post 4's argument was that the moment might be imaginary — that "AI-native language" was a framing I'd talked myself into from one narrow seat, reviewing too much generated Rust and generalizing from a headache. A second team, with a different backend, a different syntax, a different community, and no contact with me, independently converging on flat top-level structure, fits-in-context distribution, and compiler-driven constrained decoding is exactly the kind of evidence you cannot manufacture out of your own essays. The moment is real. The category is forming in real time, in public, with more than one entrant. MoonBit being early and strong de-risks the *direction* for everyone working in it, me included.

What it does not de-risk is the specific bet that's still mine to prove. MoonBit is the general-purpose, own-backend embodiment of the AI-native idea. RSScript is the review-first, lower-to-Rust embodiment. These are not the same product, and they barely compete for the same user. The open question — the one no amount of writing settles — is whether "review is the bottleneck" is a sharp enough wedge to carve out a distinct, defensible thing next to a strong general-purpose AI-native language, or whether it's a feature that the general-purpose winner eventually absorbs. I don't know. That's honest, and it's the same honest refrain this series has carried since post 1: the words are a hypothesis, and only working software, in front of people who do or don't reach for it, is evidence.

So the update to post 4's prediction is simply this: the well-resourced AI-native language arrived earlier than "within two years," it validated more of the direction than I dared expect — including shipping the layer I'd filed under "frontier" — and it competes on a different axis than the one RSScript is staking out. For a small project betting on a new category, that is about the best news there is. The category is real. There's still an unworked seat at the table. And the next thing I owe this series is not another argument — it's the first real program I actually built in the language, and what reviewing it taught me.
